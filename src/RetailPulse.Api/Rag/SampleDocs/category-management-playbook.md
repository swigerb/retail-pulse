# Category Management Playbook

## Category Roles: Defining Strategic Purpose

Every category in a retailer's assortment should be assigned a strategic role that guides investment decisions, space allocation, and pricing posture. The four standard roles are **Destination** (traffic drivers that define the banner's identity — e.g., fresh produce for a grocer), **Routine** (everyday replenishment categories like dairy and household cleaners that shoppers expect to find), **Seasonal** (time-bound categories such as grilling supplies or back-to-school that peak during specific windows), and **Convenience** (impulse or fill-in categories like single-serve beverages and checkout snacks). Assigning roles creates a common language between merchants and suppliers and ensures that limited resources — shelf space, promotional dollars, labor hours — flow to the categories that matter most for the retailer's strategic positioning.

## Assortment Optimization: The 80/20 Rule

Pareto analysis is the starting point for any assortment review. In most categories, roughly 20 % of SKUs generate 80 % of sales and margin. The long tail of underperforming items consumes shelf space, ties up working capital, and increases operational complexity without proportional return. A disciplined assortment process scores every SKU on a composite of velocity, margin contribution, household penetration, and incrementality (i.e., would the shopper simply switch to another item if this one were delisted?). Retailers who conduct quarterly assortment reviews typically reduce SKU counts by 10–15 % while holding or growing category sales, because the freed shelf space can be reallocated to facings of top movers — reducing out-of-stocks and improving the shopper's visual experience.

## Pricing Architecture: Good-Better-Best

A well-structured pricing ladder gives shoppers a clear reason to trade up while preserving an entry-level option that maintains price perception. The **Good** tier (typically private label or value brands) anchors the category's opening price point and serves price-sensitive shoppers. The **Better** tier (mainstream national brands) represents the largest share of volume and is the competitive battleground where everyday pricing and promotional frequency matter most. The **Best** tier (premium, organic, or specialty items) drives margin accretion and caters to shoppers willing to pay for quality, provenance, or unique attributes. Maintaining clear price gaps between tiers — generally 15–25 % per step — prevents cannibalization and gives the shopper a visible reason to trade up. Retailers should audit their pricing architecture quarterly to ensure gaps remain consistent as costs and competitive prices shift.

## Shelf Space Allocation

Space-to-sales analysis is the foundation of planogram productivity. Each SKU's share of shelf should be proportional to its share of category sales, adjusted for minimum facings required to maintain shelf presence and replenishment efficiency. High-velocity items often need extra facings to reduce restocking frequency and prevent out-of-stocks, while slow movers can be reduced to a single facing or moved to a less prominent position. Vertical blocking by brand or segment makes the shelf easier to shop, while horizontal flow should follow the shopper's natural scan pattern — generally left to right, top to bottom, with premium items at eye level and value items on lower shelves. The goal is a planogram that maximizes sales per linear foot while minimizing the labor cost of restocking and the lost sales from empty shelf positions.
